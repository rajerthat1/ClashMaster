How a Clash Royale AI Works [04:49]

Slide 5

To explain the project, I first have to introduce the basic working principle of a Clash Royale AI.

Game-state input
(a) All entities in the game (troops, buildings, princess/king towers, spells and projectiles) and each
entity's attributes (position, health, aggro/targeting, etc.; 47 in total), plus hero abilities.
(b) The card currently being deployed, the player's own hand and deck-cycle status (inferred by the
program), and the opponent's played-card history (used to infer the opponent's deck cycle).
(c) The player's own and the opponent's elixir, the elapsed match time, the elixir multiplier, the Royal
Chef tower progress bar (厨师塔进度条), and the current crown count.

Decisions
The model makes one decision every 250 ms: Should a card be played? Which card? At which position?
Some of the inputs above (shown in bold) may be controversial, notably the elixir counts and the inferred opponent
hand. Top human players can also do this mentally, but some will still say the AI is cheating. This is exactly what the
model's input is, and the author does not want to hide it; viewers can judge for themselves. In the future the author
may change the inputs so the model computes more itself and reads less.

Model Architecture [05:56]

Slide 6

The diagram on the left is the full version; you do not need to understand it. (The author promises no
such obscure diagrams will appear again.)

The rough flow, in six steps
1. Load card data.
2. Organize unit information.
3. Use 6 attention layers to infer relationships between units.
4. Combine the information of the whole arena (via a convolutional neural network) to obtain spatial
awareness of the arena.
5. Compress the current situation into one vector and obtain historical memory (via an LSTM).
6. Make decisions:
(a) decide whether to play a card and whether to press the ability;
(b) score each card in hand and choose which to play, or none;
(c) score every cell of the arena and choose the final deployment position.

Auxiliary prediction targets
The model additionally predicts the current win rate, the interval until the next card play, and the
time until the next play; the narration also lists which card will be played next. These help the model

Reason given in the narration: in imitation learning, 96% or more of actions are simply ``wait.'' Without auxiliary targets

Training Pipeline, Part 1: Simulating Matches [07:33]

Slide 7

Part 1 --- the prerequisite: how to simulate large numbers of Clash Royale matches accurately so
the model can gather experience.
May 2024: Bilibili user @wty-yy.
Used image recognition to train a reinforcement-learning model to play Fast Hog. With only 8 hours of
the author's own expert gameplay data, it applied offline reinforcement learning. This was pioneering,
but experience gathering was limited and performance was mediocre; its win rate against 8,000-trophy
bot opponents was low.
March--July 2026: the author's own simulator.
The author hand-wrote a Clash Royale battle environment to simulate matches (shown in an earlier
video). With so many cards, evolutions (觉醒) and balance changes, the workload was too large and
the accuracy of card interactions was low, so this project hit an obvious bottleneck.
August 2026: reverse-engineering Nulls Royale.
The author reverse-engineered the Clash Royale private server Nulls Royale and could read game state
directly from the Android process, including unit positions and health. From this video on, training an
AI went from completely infeasible to somewhat feasible: the program finally had a window into the
battle engine.
September 2026: FirstlightCR.
FirstlightCR appeared, by Bilibili creator @真的无名呢. He completely reverse-engineered the Nulls
Royale battle-engine interface and can read almost every battle-related detail accurately. For the first
time a model could gather large amounts of experience from accurate matches on the native engine;
it ultimately reached the private server's Hall of Fame and laid the foundation for Clash Royale AI
training methods.

Training Pipeline, Part 1 (continued) [09:34]

Slide 8

How can the model gather experience as fast as possible?
• Firstlight's method. Plant a ``mole'' (内鬼) in the Nulls Royale client. It hides in the game process,
reads memory, and sends it over the network to an external listener program; the external program
can send commands in to control actions such as card placement. This requires the computer to
run an Android emulator; without one the game process cannot run and simulation cannot start.
• The author's constraint. The compute he can use is an x86_64 Linux system without KVM virtualization, meaning it cannot run virtual machines or an Android emulator.
• The breakthrough. His collaborator Arron came up with an excellent idea: remove the emulator
shell altogether and make the game engine run directly on Linux and talk directly to the program
(a Python program, per the narration). He succeeded.
• Result. Experience-gathering efficiency greatly exceeded Firstlight's. With the existing compute,
simulating the engine alone yields 140,000 matches per hour, i.e. about 20 years of Clash Royale
matches per day.

Training Pipeline, Part 2: Imitation Learning [10:45]

Slide 9

Part 2 --- the key to training: imitation learning.
Once data collection and accurate battle simulation are no longer bottlenecks, how to train the AI
becomes the most important question.
For a full month, before Firstlight appeared, the author tried to train an AI from scratch using his handbuilt Clash Royale engine (see his earlier video) and failed. The model's policy collapsed quickly
from the very start of training and it completely abandoned the use of tank (胖子) and spell cards (a
misplaced or mistimed play loses elixir and directly causes a loss), stalling in a local optimum.
• Why: trial and error punishes the model too severely. If a tank or spell is played imperfectly, the
opponent can easily answer it, for example with a Mini P.E.K.K.A (the narration also says ``or air'',
空气), and the model loses elixir heavily and then the game. So the model's first lesson is to use
these cards less, and it gradually uses them less and less until it never uses them. It never learned
to attack with a tank or to use a spell to sweep an enemy Archer pair.
• ``Trial and error from scratch is simply too slow.''
• Self-play can fall into a local optimum: the opponent is weak and I am weak, we both flail around,
and it feels fine.
Why did firstlight-cr far exceed the author's own research? The secret is imitating the matches of top
human players: Firstlight first had its model imitate 240,000 human expert matches, avoiding blind
trial and error from scratch. The author says he regretted it deeply after seeing that video; with his
own simulator he had no way of having the program reproduce human expert matches, so Firstlight's
approach was highly forward-looking.

Training Pipeline, Part 2 (continued) [12:18]

Slide 10

Imitation learning has two big steps.
1. RoyaleAPI replay conversion and validation. The original replay files are very small; they only
record when each side placed cards and used abilities (discrete actions, with the game tick). Conversion means following those discrete actions to rebuild the entire match with the battle engine
and storing it in a format the model can read. Validation means checking the final result of every
match, making sure tower health matches what the replay file recorded, so the model never learns
from an incorrect situation or play.
2. Actual imitation learning: the model learns how humans play. This takes roughly 10 hours of
training (per narration). Daybreak CR's imitation results:

Training Pipeline, Part 3: Deep Reinforcement Learning [13:20]

• After a series of optimizations, training throughput reached 33,000 matches per hour.
• League training:
– Deck sampling. In every match, both sides' decks are randomly sampled from the decks of human
matches. For example, if 2% of matches use Giant Skeleton Hammer, the model has a 2% chance
of being given that deck; if 4% use Fast Hog, a 4% chance.
– Opponents. Half of the opponents are the model itself (self-play); the other half are several of
the model's earlier checkpoints. The lower the model's relative win rate against a checkpoint,
the larger that checkpoint's share.
– Updates. Every 1,000 matches the weights are updated with the PPO algorithm; every 20 updates a new checkpoint is generated.
– Champion rule. If a checkpoint wins more than 55% against the current ``champion'' over 800
matches, with no large deterioration on other metrics, it becomes the new champion. If it is
below 45%, training rolls back. If 10 consecutive checkpoints produce no new champion, the
loop stops and the program ends (a sign the model has hit a bottleneck).
• The model that reached around rank 3000 went through 360 PPO updates, i.e. 360,000 matches.

Key Training Takeaways, Part 1: Details Decide [15:06]

Slide 12

The training process is simply imitation learning plus reinforcement learning, with nothing academically innovative. But every stage has many details, and every one is crucial: any oversight makes the
model learn the wrong thing and performance drops sharply. Three of the bugs the author met (all
now fixed, though see the third):
1. Temperature. Training used Temperature = 1.0, which is fine, but in real-match testing Temperature = 0.05 was found to make the model much stronger. If you also test with Temperature = 1, the
model looks terrible, but it is not actually that bad.
2. Memory reader missing targeting. A small error in the memory-reading program meant each
entity's target lock (索敌) could not be read, so the model played blind. After the fix the model went
from about rank 5000 to about rank 3000 on the international server, a very large improvement.
3. Headless engine card-placement logic. There is a forced 1.1 s delay between tapping the screen
and the card actually being deployed, and during that window the engine wrongly rejected all
subsequent card-placement events, so the interval between plays was forced to 1.1 s. This hurts
low-cost play (which needs fast card cycling), so the model still does not play low-cost decks well.
It can only be fixed in the next training run, which is now under way.

Key Training Takeaways, Part 3: Search [18:50]

Slide 14

Search is the next thing a Clash Royale AI needs, and the only way for a model to surpass the ``Pharaoh''
(法老; the video does not explain the term). Only one last decisive breakthrough remains. To exceed human strategy the model must use its advantages (counting elixir, tracking cards, running huge numbers
of simulations) to find strategies better than a human's.
• Search is possible. Because training is free from the Android emulator, the author implemented
fork-based search for the battle engine. Fork the original process; the two engine versions have
identical, complete game state, and from that position they can branch to explore what different
card plays lead to. It is a bit slow, but with many such game processes, experience gathering is still
quick overall.
• Search is effective. Before the model places a card, re-ranking its candidate positions by search
and playing against the same model without search raises the win rate to about 70% (from roughly
50--50 to 70--30).
• But the model struggles to learn search's choices. After accumulating many searched labels, the
model can only memorize the specific correct answers in the training set; the knowledge does not
generalize to unseen matches, so distillation efficiency is extremely low. The author suggests two
possible reasons:
(a) Not enough data. Search collects data much more slowly than self-play; with very much more
data the model might learn better choices.
(b) The model lacks real understanding of Clash Royale and cannot do internal reasoning. This
shows in the critic's very low explained variance, only about 0.3: its judgment of a position
is inaccurate, so its evaluation after a search is also inaccurate and the labels themselves are
imperfect.

Open-Source Policy [23:15]

Slide 17

The whole Clash Royale AI training pipeline has basically been made to work. The author has opensourced the complete training code (imitation learning, reinforcement learning, and replay conversion) at https://github.com/Jason-XII/daybreak-cr. However, some dependencies cannot
be open-sourced for now, and he does not dare to.
1. The model weights cannot be released. If they leaked, the consequences would be serious and
hard to predict. Academically, training on top of this model would save a great deal of time on
imitation and reinforcement learning, whether fine-tuning or retraining, and progress would be
much faster. But would people really use the model only for AI research? The author thinks not. If
the weights were released, the model would become the strongest Clash Royale cheat ever, which
would also hit the paid-boosting industry (代打). From an ordinary player's point of view, he wants
the opponent at the start of every match to be a real person like himself, not a cold machine that
reliably plays the optimal move, and not to have to wonder whether the opponent is a cheating bot
or a boosted account.
He ran his international-server tests only to measure the model's ability, with a small, limited number of matches. So Daybreak CR is currently in closed internal testing: you can challenge the AI
on the private server, but the weights will not be public for now. Nulls Royale clan: Daybreak CR
(search that keyword).
2. The reverse-engineering principle of the headless engine will not be published. The author asks
for everyone's understanding.
